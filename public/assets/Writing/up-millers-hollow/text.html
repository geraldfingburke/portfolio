I think I turn left here. I almost miss the turn and my back tires slide a bit as I push through the turn much faster than I’d meant to. It had been a long time since I’d been in this little slice of country and my internal compass was rusty. Being perfectly honest, I didn’t want to be driving down that particular road at that particular moment, but the things we want to do and the things we have to do have a funny way of never overlapping. I put my high-beams on, illuminating the trees to either side and above the narrow road, the winnowed winter branches reaching leafless across the road casting strange shadows against the mountainous backdrop. Things like this seldom bothered me before I left the hollow and moved out to the city, but it was different now. The darkness creeps into my skin in a way it didn’t used to. It’s not just the absence of light, but the suggestion of some unseen force liberated by the darkness. I shudder and try to shift my focus back to the road. There’s only one house up Miller’s Hollow, and you might be forgiven for thinking a Miller owns it. I know I assumed much the same as a child. But my family said that the name of the road and the hollow itself come from a wealthy family that left the area a long time ago. Years before the factories started to close and the mines started to dry up. The town was dead and the Miller’s weren’t even there to witness it. Instead, it was my family that owned the 3 bedroom home up Miller’s Hollow. The decrepit white farmhouse with the wraparound porch my grandfather was said to have built with his own two hands. When my grandfather died, his knowledge of carpentry and anything else potentially useful to a home went with him. In his stead was my father, drunk and indolent. The man never lifted a finger out of anything other than violence. But even he died when I was still a kid. Drank his brain to boiling and drooled himself to death in his sleep. Lord knows my mother tried, but she had to work so much that things around the house just got neglected. By the time I was a teenager, the home had fallen into such disrepair, my grandma told my mother to rent us an apartment or she’d have the city condemn the place. I always loved my grandma. She did the hard thing no one wanted to do and said the hard things no one wanted to say. If her hands hadn’t been folded into wads by arthritis, I’d be willing to bet she’d have done the maintenance work herself. But that sad little house sat empty, rotting on the side of the mountain for years. I’d almost forgotten about it. From time to time, I’d brag to my school friends about my family owning land up Miller’s Hollow. When you grew up poor in a poor community, the bar was pretty low. One time, in high school, I took some friends up to the old house. I could barely stomach the memories and my friends just hung around breaking windows until I could convince them to leave. It was the last time I’d seen the house. But now, I was going to see it again. Hopefully, for the last time. The first thing my headlights caught was the plastic reflector on the side of the mailbox. During heavy rains, the mile-long gravel driveway would wash out. The post office refused to deliver our mail until we moved the box to the end of the paved road. Even the rugged SUVs the rural carriers would employ were no match for the driveway in slick weather. When I flew into the regional airport, I made a point to rent something with four-wheel drive. Even that might not be enough to help me. The transition from asphalt to the decades old suggestion of gravel stamped into long unturned turf was jarring and I felt the suspension moan under the shock. I slowed down and got into low gear, praying I wouldn’t slide off into the ditch and have to make good on the rental agency’s roadside assistance. They would have to call a local firm to tow and I wouldn’t be surprised if every one of them didn’t hang up when they heard the words “Miller’s Hollow”. The going was bumpy, but much better than I could have expected. The summer had been dry, and though more gravel was washed into the ditches on either side of the road, the dirt was hard-packed enough to prevent slippage. I slowed my approach as the memories of growing up in that house flooded my mind. It overwhelmed me and I found myself stopping a few hundred feet short of the end of the driveway that terminated along the side of the house. I looked down at my phone. No reception. It seems crazy to think that the last time I’d been here, smart phones didn’t exist. Hell, most of the teens in my podunk town didn’t even have the Razer flip-phones that had become a staple of Myspace selfies in my high school years. I opened up the folder that archived my text messages and read back the words that brought me here in the first place. Your father came back last night. We need to talk. Meet me at the old house up Miller’s Hollow. I thought the message was some kind of weird prank when I first read it. It had come from my mother’s number, but it didn’t seem like the kind of joke she would play. She was a shrewd, hard woman. She had her own sense of humor, but she didn’t get much out of pranks or hijinks. When I tried to message her back, the message showed as undeliverable. I tried to call her, but her number was out of service. We didn’t talk a lot, but enough that I would have been notified if she’d changed her number. I called my brother and sister, but neither of them knew any more than I did. They hadn’t received the cryptic message, but they couldn’t get ahold of her when they reached out either. I messaged a few of my mom’s friends that I still had on Facebook, but all of them mentioned not having been able to get a hold of her in the past few weeks. They seemed to have just assumed she had taken one of her reclusive spells. One of them went as far as to drop by her house, but her car was gone and no one answered the phone. As I neared the end of the driveway, the reflected light bouncing back off a mirror surface told me that her car was here. That she was here. There was no light coming from the house, but I expected that. The utilities company came out the last day we were moving and we had to finish the last few loads with flashlights hanging out of our mouths. I turned off my engine, cut off my lights, and watched closely for any sign of light or movement. Only the reflection of the moon shone in any of the windows. I sometimes wonder why I didn’t turn around and go back right then. Drive the car back to the rental place. Grab an overnight flight back to the city and just leave this. But there was a part of me that saw a cry for help in the message she sent me. Further evidenced by her disconnecting from her entire support network. She needed me and there would always be a part of me that felt like I owed her something for making it work after my dad died. I got out of the car slowly, the realities of the six-hour flight and two-hour drive coming all at once in my staggered step. It suddenly occurred to me that I ached all over and it was only the adrenaline pumping through my veins that I was able to carry myself forward at all. I swung the door shut, loud, and waited for some kind of reaction. A skittering down the stairs, a “oh, honey, you’re here”, but there was just the country silence of a thousand insects screeching around a teeming wood. I carried myself up to the half-white washed steps of the front porch and froze for a moment when I realized I could hear a faint creaking. It sounded like it was coming from inside the home. I noticed that the netted screen door was closed, but the large, oak front door was wide open. The netting of the screen obscured the void and I couldn’t distinguish any features within. I called out “Hello”, and the creaking came to an abrupt halt. The front door opened up to a set of stairs that ran up to a landing on the second floor. From the landing, I could hear a series of loud banging sounds, and something else. Something like the sound it makes when you rub the ends of two broken sticks together. Dread filled the pit of my stomach and I found myself leaping from the porch as something dark against the shadows tumbled down the stairs. I ran, out of instinct, to my mother’s old burgundy Buick LeSabre and tried the door. It refused to budge and I briefly looked through the window. It was all I could do to keep myself from fainting. The sound of the screen door slamming open brought me to my senses and I ran to the rental car, my feet barely touching the ground. I slammed the door shut, locked it, and turned the engine over in what seemed like a single motion. I looked up as I dropped into reverse and stopped. I had expected to see the thing that was chasing me clawing at my window, but it was much worse. My headlights illuminated a scene directly from my childhood. My dad sat in his rocking chair, holding a green glass bottle of rolling rock. My grandfather and grandmother sat on a small wooden bench. My grandfather whittled a small block of wood and my grandmother sat with one of the trashy romance novels I had always seen her with. But the worst of them all was my Mother. She leaned against the porch railing gazing wistfully into the night. Everyone on that porch was dead. Everyone, including my mother. I backed out into the grass, turned and headed back down the gravel road. I was halfway back to town before I could get cell service. I called my friend from school who now worked in the sheriff’s office and they helped me through the process. As much as I didn’t want to, I had to lead the coroner back to the place where I had found my mother. I was relieved when they agreed to wait until the light of day to make the trip. An autopsy showed a massive growth in her chest. The family practice she had gone to for years mentioned her having some abnormal results in a blood test and recommending her to a specialist in the city, but they hadn’t heard from her since. Foul play wasn’t considered and the sheriff’s office thought little of the cryptic message I had received. I left out the part about seeing my dead family on the porch, for fear of being labeled a nut job. That was ten years ago. A few months ago, I was diagnosed with something rare and aggressive. The prognosis wasn’t good to begin with, but after a round or two of treatment, I was told any further care would be palliative. I can’t describe how waiting to die feels, so I’m not going to try. What I will say is that I received a letter this morning. The envelope had handwriting on it that I would have recognized blindfolded. When I opened it up, all it contained was a single neatly folded piece of paper. The words “It’s time to come home” were written in my mother’s neat, no-nonsense lettering. I sent my sister a message this morning, asking her to meet me at the house. I spent a few days getting my affairs in order and the last thing on my list was canceling my phone. I called the phone company as soon as I contacted my sister. It’s funny how the things your parents always did started to make sense as you got older. I can’t say why my soul belongs to a home that served as a backdrop for some of the worst moments in my life. But I suppose you can choose your home as well as you can choose your family. No matter how deep you cut, some bonds are just too strong to sever. So here I go, taking one last run up Miller’s Hollow.
