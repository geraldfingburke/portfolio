Three weeks ago, there was a fire at my uncle's house. The fire department's investigation concluded that it was caused by faulty wiring in the wall. My cousin woke up and noticed the fire, ushering her parents and her little brother out of the house. The family calls her our "little hero". Everyone managed to get out safely, even their geriatric dog. But, for a reason no one has been able to comprehend, my uncle went back in. In the chaos, my aunt lost track of him and was only able to conjure a feeble plea when she saw him walking back through the door. He never came out.

After the fire was extinguished, they were able to recover my uncle's body. They found him lying on the smoldering remains of his bed. Some in the family have speculated it was suicide. Others believe he was delirious, not in full control of his actions. My cousin even suggested, during a quiet moment at the funeral, that he may have gone back in to get his phone, which was often plugged in next to his bed. All of this speculation, by my estimation, was pointless. The simple fact was, he had gone back in and he hadn't come out. He was gone.

While we were never close, this was the first time I had lost a member of my immediate family. The bizarre gap his absence created and the weight it placed on my relationships with my aunt and cousins was more than I could bear. I began to withdraw from my family and focus on my life as an independent 20-something. My independence was supported in part by the mountain of student loan money I would borrow each semester and by doing gig work. Delivering food, shopping for groceries. I even got an app that saw me delivering batches of packages from the online retailer you think of when you think of online retailers. Given the nature of what I'm writing here, I'd like to avoid getting too specific with corporate entities.

Living in a college town, tips for orders can be hit or miss. Your average broke college student tends to be more generous at the beginning of a semester when they're feeling flush. By the time finals start to come up, the pickings get slim. I often find myself working later and later just to hit my daily goals so I can indulge in life's luxuries, like rent and electricity. This is why, last night, I found myself literally jumping for joy when I got a grocery order with an estimated tip over one hundred dollars. The cynical part of my mind wrote it off as tip-bait. A practice where a customer promises a large tip to get priority treatment, only to remove the tip from the final pay. Whether or not it was too good to be true, I couldn't afford to pass up the potential opportunity. I hit accept, astonished I'd managed to get it before another desperate drive swooped in.

The order was for a nearby grocery store. When I arrived, I grabbed my cart and checked the shopping list. A dozen gallons of spring water. I had picked up some strange orders before, but this and the high tip had stoked my curiosity a great deal. As I loaded the water, I tried to imagine the doomsday prepper whose bunker I'd be delivering to. I checked out and loaded the water into my car. The app gave me the address and I punched it into my GPS. There was something odd about the address, but I couldn't put my finger on it. As I approached the last quarter mile stretch, I realized what it was.

"You have arrived" my GPS chimed, as I pulled directly in front of the burned-out husk that was my uncle's home. A deep well of emotion rose in me as I read the house number listed in the app from the side of my late uncle's mailbox. There must be some kind of mistake, I thought. I pulled up the app and looked at the customer's name. Harold M. My gut churned. The name meant nothing to me when it first appeared on my screen, but now, seeing it on my phone screen while I sat in front of the place where he died filled me with dread.

This is a prank. Someone is playing a prank on me. I pressed the phone icon next to the customer's name, prepared to give them a piece of my mind for the dark and ill-timed joke. As I listened to the dial tone on my end, my ears began to pick something up. Faintly, in the direction of my uncle's former home, wafting over the blackened beams and caution tape was a ringtone. My uncle's ringtone. I looked in the direction of the ringing phone long enough to see a dark shape moving against the blackened timber and I put my car in drive and drove faster than I've driven in my life.

I didn't stop until I got to my dorm. I marked the order as "undeliverable" and was advised to donate or dispose of the water. I couldn't bring myself to remove it from my trunk. I called my roommate and had them walk me from my car to our shared apartment. I couldn't bring myself to tell them what happened, so I made an excuse about having a bad customer and locked myself in my bedroom for the rest of the evening.

I didn't go to class today, and I have no intention of working this evening. I'm going to have to wait until I'm less spooked about everything. The thought of even getting back in my car and going out there is too much for me. It's the reason I ordered dinner from one of the apps I happen to drive for, in spite of the absolutely insane markup to do so. It's also the reason I'm writing this.

About ten minutes after placing my order, I got a notification that Harold M. was going to be my driver. I know it could be a coincidence, but after what happened last night, I'm not sure. I sent him a message requesting a contact free delivery. Some drivers will make a brief courtesy knock to let you know your food has arrived. This driver has been knocking for the past ten minutes and I don't know how much longer I can ignore it.
